How long do India's district courts take? Measuring, explaining and forecasting the backlog

Arnav Goel · github.com/ArnavGoel98/CourtSight

Download PDF

Abstract. India's district and subordinate courts held {{pending_last}} crore pending cases at the end of {{last}}, up from {{pending_first}} crore in {{first}}. We combine the government's state-wise pending and disposal counts (2014–{{last}}) with {{cases_m}} million case records from {{districts_ttd}} districts (filed 2010–2018). Four results follow. (i) Kaplan–Meier estimates that account for still-pending cases put the median time to a decision at {{civ_med}} months for an ordinary civil suit, with {{civ_5y}}% still undecided after five years. (ii) The cases filed in 2010–2018 spent {{cy_all}} crore case-years waiting; the {{pend_cr}} crore still undecided at collection had a median age of {{age_med}} years. (iii) COVID added an estimated {{covid21}} lakh cases ({{covid21_lo}}–{{covid21_hi}} lakh) to the backlog by the end of 2021, calibrated against the projection method's own pre-COVID errors. (iv) Across districts, caseload per courtroom barely predicts slowness, and structural features explain only {{r2s}}% of the variation. Two negative results are reported in full: a judge-handover design fails its placebo test, and a district-level forecasting model ties, rather than beats, simple official-series forecasts for 2019. A forecast of every state's pending count on 31 December 2026 was locked by hash before the outcome exists.

1. Introduction

Delay is the most visible failure of Indian courts, and the least well measured. Official dashboards report how many cases are pending, but not how long a case takes: a pending count mixes old and new cases, and averages over decided cases alone leave out exactly the slow ones that are still waiting. This paper measures delay with methods that handle unfinished cases, puts a number on the total time spent waiting, separates the COVID shock from the trend that was already under way, and asks what slow districts have in common. It also commits to a forecast that can be checked.

The emphasis throughout is on claims that can be falsified. Each estimate is paired with a benchmark or a placebo test, the results that failed are reported alongside those that held, and every figure is rebuilt from the public repository by continuous integration.

2. Data

Official series. State-wise pending cases at 31 December for 2014–{{last}} and disposals for 2014–2021, transcribed from the Ministry of Law and Justice's answers to Lok Sabha questions (USQ 1838, 16 December 2022; USQ 2362, 13 February 2026), which in turn draw on the National Judicial Data Grid. The state figures are tested against the printed national totals; Kerala's 2016 disposal figure, roughly ten times its neighbouring years, fails and is excluded. Filings are never published for all years, so they are recovered from the stock–flow identity filedt = Pt − Pt−1 + Dt.

Case records. The Development Data Lab's eCourts extract (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): {{cases_m}} million cases filed between January 2010 and December 2018, with filing date, decision date (if any), case type, court and district. Each state was collected at a different time, between {{coll_first}} and {{coll_last}}. A state's collection month is defined as the last month in which recorded decisions reach 20% of its 2018 monthly average; later decision dates, including typographical ones such as the year 9201, are treated as undecided. Case types are grouped into ten categories from the free-text names each court uses. The judge table gives each judge's courtroom and start and end dates.

What is published. Only aggregates: counts by district, filing month and decision month. No case-level rows and no names of people leave the analysis.

3. How long does a case take?

For each district and case type, the time from filing to any final disposal is estimated with the Kaplan–Meier product-limit estimator, Ŝ(t) = ∏tᵢ ≤ t(1 − dᵢ/nᵢ), at monthly resolution. Cases still undecided at the collection month are right-censored there, so slow cases contribute the time they are known to have waited instead of being dropped. Intervals use Greenwood's variance. "Decided" means any disposal, including dismissal, withdrawal, settlement and transfer.

The ordering is stable and large. Bail applications are decided within weeks. Family cases take a median of {{fam_med}} months. Civil suits are the slowest major category: median {{civ_med}} months, and {{civ_5y}}% are still pending five years after filing. Across all civil cases the median is {{civil_med}} months, against {{crim_med}} for criminal cases, but the criminal tail is as long: {{crim_5y}}% of criminal cases are still pending after five years. District-level curves for 626 districts are published in the interactive calculator, in English and Hindi.

4. Years of waiting

A pending count treats a case filed last month and a case filed eight years ago alike. Summing the age of each pending case at collection gives a measure of accumulated delay. Of the {{cases_m}} million cases filed in 2010–2018, {{pend_cr}} crore were still undecided when their state's data was collected. Together they had waited {{cy_pending}} crore case-years; adding the time spent by the cases decided before collection gives {{cy_all}} crore case-years. The median pending case had waited {{age_med}} years (mean {{age_mean}}); {{over3}}% had waited more than three years and {{over5}}% more than five. These are lower bounds: cases filed before 2010 are not in the data, and most cases involve two or more people.

5. The COVID shock

The counterfactual for each state projects its mean log growth over 2015–2019 forward from 2019, a rule fixed before post-2019 data was examined. In backtests from 2016–2018 origins, this projection under-predicted a backlog that was already accelerating (national error between about −3% and +2% per year of horizon). The headline counterfactual is corrected by the median backtest error, and its range spans the backtest errors, so the stated uncertainty is the method's own observed error rather than a model of it. By the end of 2021 the backlog stood {{covid21}} lakh above the counterfactual (range {{covid21_lo}}–{{covid21_hi}} lakh), {{covid21_share}}% of the stock. The gap narrows after 2022, and by 2025 the range includes zero.

At the 2023–2025 pace, the national backlog grows by about {{per_year}} lakh cases a year and does not clear; clearing it within ten years would take about {{clear10}} lakh extra decisions every year, {{clear10_pct}}% more than courts decided in 2021.

6. What do slow districts have in common?

For {{drv_n}} districts with at least 2,000 cases, the outcome is the share of civil cases still pending three years after filing (Kaplan–Meier). It ranges from {{drv_p10}}% in the fastest tenth of districts to {{drv_p90}}% in the slowest. We regress it on standardised district features computed from the case and judge records for 2014–2017: caseload per active courtroom (log), district size (log annual filings), the criminal share of filings, and the median months a judge stays in one courtroom. The 95% intervals come from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of districts.

Caseload per courtroom, the usual explanation, barely predicts slowness ({{load_b}} percentage points per standard deviation, interval {{load_lo}} to {{load_hi}}). Longer judge tenure goes with faster courts ({{ten_b}} points per SD of about {{ten_sd}} months), and a heavier criminal docket with slower civil cases ({{crim_b}} points). Together these features explain only {{r2s}}% of the variance between districts. A throughput variable (decisions ÷ filings) raises this to {{r2t}}%, but it is close to a restatement of the outcome and is not interpreted. These are cross-sectional correlations: districts with longer-serving judges may differ in many other ways, and most of what makes a district slow (local practice, adjournments, vacancies, the bar) is not recorded in this data.

7. Does a change of judge slow a courtroom? A failed design

Judges in India are transferred often, and a new judge must learn a docket. We built a stacked difference-in-differences event study around {{j_events}} judge handovers in {{j_districts}} districts: each courtroom's monthly disposals relative to its own previous twelve months, minus the same for same-district courtrooms without a change, with a district block bootstrap. A planted synthetic −30% effect is recovered. On real data, disposals are about {{j_true}}% lower in the year after a handover.

The design does not pass a placebo test. Moving every handover 18 or 30 months earlier, to dates when no judge changed, still yields drops of {{j_p18}}% and {{j_p30}}%. The decline is under way before the new judge arrives; the comparison picks up courtrooms that were already slowing. We therefore report no estimate. A further limitation: courtroom numbers in the judge and case tables agree in only {{j_link}}% of judge-months.

8. Forecasting, and grading forecasts honestly

2019, graded. A district-level LightGBM quantile model trained on the case records beats the best simple forecast by {{skill_lo}}–{{skill_hi}}% (pinball loss) on held-out 2016 and 2017 origins. Its 2019 state forecasts were committed before any 2019 outcome was examined and then scored against the official figures for 27 states. At state level it matches simple forecasts built from the official series (mean absolute error of backlog growth 0.077, against 0.075 for a common rate and 0.076 for repeating 2018) but does not beat them. We report this as a tie.

2026, locked. Pending cases on 31 December 2026, for every state, were forecast from data through 2025 with a method chosen by a rule fixed before any backtest (lowest case-weighted error of one-year state log growth across six target years). The national median forecast is {{f50}} crore (10–90%: {{f10}}–{{f90}} crore). 9. Limitations

  • No public case-level data exists after 2018. The official 2023–2025 series has no disposal figures, and some state series jump in single years (Tripura −75%, Uttarakhand −17%).
  • "Decided" includes dismissals, withdrawals and transfers; a transfer can end one case record and open another.
  • Case-type grouping relies on court-specific names; 9% of cases fall in "other".
  • Nothing here identifies how many judges would be needed, or what would happen if a policy changed. The district analysis is descriptive, and the one causal design attempted failed.
  • High Courts and the Supreme Court are not covered.

10. Conclusion

Measured properly, delay in India's district courts is longer than averages over decided cases suggest, and the backlog was growing before COVID and is still growing after it. The variation between districts is large and is mostly not explained by caseload per courtroom, which argues for studying how courts work, not just how many there are. The methods, data aggregates, interactive tools and a forecast waiting to be graded are public, so each claim here can be checked and, if wrong, corrected.
