      How to read this map, and what it cannot show

      Shapes are 2011 Census districts. Court districts have changed since. Districts carved out after 2011 are shown inside their 2011 parent, with both courts' cases pooled. Cities whose courts are recorded as several units (Jaipur, Jodhpur) or as city-wide courts (Mumbai) are pooled the same way. The tooltip lists every court unit behind a shape, and the crosswalk is published (sources/map_crosswalk.csv).

      Some districts are withheld. In several court units, including Gaya, Muzaffarpur, Bhojpur and Vaishali in Bihar and Chandauli in Uttar Pradesh, the records hold almost no decisions before 2018. Every case there would look stuck, so these units would have topped the "slowest" list. They are hatched instead, and the tooltip says why.

      Other hatched areas have no court records in this data: most of Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, Andaman & Nicobar, Puducherry and Lakshadweep. That is a gap in the eCourts records for 2010–2018, not a fast or a slow court.

      Colours are fifths. Each colour holds about a fifth of the shapes, so the map shows rank, not size. Check the legend: for some case types the slowest fifth and the fastest fifth differ by only a few points. Small districts have wide uncertainty. Where two districts' rank ranges overlap on the rankings page, they are not clearly different.

      The records are incomplete. For 2015–2018, the eCourts records hold about 61% of the cases the official figures say were filed, from 8% in Tripura to all of them in Bihar. The coverage layer shows this by state; states with under 2 lakh official filings in those years (Goa, Sikkim, Manipur, Mizoram, Meghalaya and the smallest union territories) are left blank there, because their official figures move too much from year to year to compare. Thinly covered states may be represented by unusual courts. See the audit.

      Pending records are mostly live cases. Nationally, 95% of cases pending at collection had a hearing listed in the year before, so a slow district is not just one that failed to record its decisions. The exceptions are West Bengal and Odisha, where 53% and 29% had not been listed for over a year; their delays may be overstated.

      Hearing layers. "Days between hearings" is the typical (geometric mean) gap between a pending case's last hearing and its next scheduled one, for cases still pending when the data was collected; "summons or appearance" is the share of those cases whose next hearing was for getting the parties to court. West Bengal, Odisha and Mizoram are hatched on these layers: many of their pending records had no hearing listed in the year before collection, so their gaps may reflect record-keeping rather than scheduling. Both come from free-text labels and dates typed by court staff (see the findings). Districts that space hearings further apart are slower, but that is a correlation.

      Descriptive only. A slow district is not necessarily a badly run one: case mix, vacancies, local practice and how cases are recorded all differ.
